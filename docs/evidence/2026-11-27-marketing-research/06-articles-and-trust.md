# Article hooks, original product education and health-marketing trust

**Bottom line.** A scraped article hook may win a moment of attention, but it is a weak and risky foundation for YFIT’s health-adjacent marketing. The more defensible strategy is to use articles as research leads, verify the underlying fact, paraphrase it in original language, and quickly answer one real user job with a working-app demonstration. That should improve *relevance and credibility* as a conversion hypothesis, but there is no evidence here that copying a hook increases response, or that the current YFIT funnel is failing. The proposed unit is: **one real user job → one useful answer → one observable app proof → one small CTA**.

## What can and cannot be reused

An online article, photograph, video, narration, music track, or distinctive headline should be treated as protected unless a clear license or permission says otherwise. The U.S. Copyright Office says permission can be requested from the owner and that there is no fixed “safe” number of words, seconds, or percentage for fair use. Fair use depends on the circumstances; in doubt, the Office recommends obtaining permission. It also distinguishes the copyright in a photograph from ownership of a physical copy, and says that changing someone else’s work does not by itself give the adapter copyright in the result.[1] Attribution is good editorial practice, but attribution alone is not permission.

For YFIT, the practical rule is simple: do not download and republish article text, screenshots, stock clips, creator video, or audio merely because it is publicly viewable. Keep a source log with URL, author, publication date, the fact being checked, and the asset license. Prefer footage recorded by YFIT, app screens actually captured from the working product, commissioned or licensed stock with commercial-social rights, and music cleared for the destination platform. If a source is essential to commentary, quote only the minimum needed, identify the source on-screen or in the caption, add substantial original analysis, and have an owner review the particular use. That is a risk-control workflow, not a legal conclusion.

Platform originality is also a distribution and trust issue. YouTube says monetized content should be original and authentic; borrowed material must be changed significantly, and mass-produced, generic, repetitive content can be ineligible for monetization. Its reused-content review is separate from copyright permission, so a clip can be licensed yet still look insufficiently original for the platform’s quality standard.[2] This supports moving away from five generic article montages toward a short, specific explanation plus visible YFIT proof. It does **not** prove that one scene always beats several cuts, nor does it establish a universal ideal length.

## Health and product-proof boundaries

Health-related marketing has a higher trust burden because viewers can reasonably infer medical or efficacy claims from an alarming hook, a before/after visual, a medication reference, or confident form-analysis language. FTC guidance says advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and that objective express or implied claims need adequate substantiation before dissemination. Health-benefit and safety claims generally require competent and reliable scientific evidence; for many efficacy claims, the guidance describes randomized, controlled human testing as the general standard.[3] The same guidance warns that marketers are responsible for implied messages, not only literal words, and that a link to third-party material does not insulate the advertiser from claims consumers may take from it.[3]

Therefore avoid claims such as “fixes pain,” “prevents injury,” “corrects your form,” “replaces a clinician,” “works for everyone,” or medication-specific promises unless there is product-specific evidence and appropriate review. Do not turn an article about a condition into a YFIT promise. A safer educational frame is narrow and observable: “Here is one way to review the movement you recorded,” “YFIT highlights this visible pattern for exploration,” or “Use this as general information, not a diagnosis; stop if movement causes pain and seek qualified care when appropriate.” Even those lines should match what the app actually does. A disclaimer cannot cure a misleading headline or unsupported implication.

“Proof” should mean proof of the product’s operation, not proof of a health outcome. Show a real, current flow: upload or record, the relevant analysis or feedback screen, and the user decision it supports. Label demo data as demo data. Do not show fabricated pain relief, injury recovery, medication outcomes, clinical credentials, or a founder speaking as though they personally experienced a result they did not. The FTC’s endorsement guidance says testimonials must be truthful and not misleading, endorsers should not describe an experience they have not had, and material connections should be disclosed.[4] A fictional founder testimonial, AI face, or synthetic patient story is especially poor proof in a health context because it can imply a real person, a real outcome, and independent validation at once.

## AI, faces, and disclosure

If synthetic media is used for illustration rather than testimony, disclose it plainly in the creative and follow the destination platform’s current controls. YouTube requires disclosure when realistic AI-generated or altered content makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not, alters a real event or place, or creates a realistic scene that did not occur; it adds a viewer label and may apply penalties for repeated non-disclosure.[5] TikTok requires labeling realistic AI-generated content and prohibits certain misleading or impersonating uses, including fake authoritative sources or endorsements; its guidance allows creator disclosure and may apply automatic labels.[6] Meta describes “AI info” labels for a wider range of detected or self-disclosed AI-generated video, audio, and images.[7]

These labels are not a substitute for honesty. A small “AI” note does not make a fake customer outcome acceptable. For YFIT, the conservative choice is real founder voice, real screen capture, real user permission, and no photorealistic synthetic person presented as a customer, clinician, or independent reviewer. If AI is used for a non-deceptive graphic or voice cleanup, disclose material use when the platform requires it and avoid making the synthetic element carry credibility.

## Recommended editorial pipeline

Start with a real job, not a viral article. Examples are hypotheses to test, not claims of demand: “I recorded a movement; what can I inspect before my next session?” or “I want a repeatable way to compare two recordings.” Write a brief that promises a useful answer in plain language. Research one or two reputable sources for background, verify the date and scope, then paraphrase the relevant fact. Keep the source for verification and attribution; do not copy the hook, structure, or distinctive phrasing. If the source is uncertain or the fact is contested, say so or omit it.

Then record the working-app proof before the CTA. A 15–30 second structure is a reasonable YFIT test hypothesis: first seconds show the user problem, next seconds give one bounded educational point, then show the real YFIT action and result, ending with “Try your own recording” or another low-pressure CTA. One focused scene is likely easier to understand than five unrelated clips, but this is a testable attention-and-comprehension hypothesis, not a guaranteed conversion lift. Stock footage can illustrate context; it should not impersonate product evidence.

Use a pre-publish gate: (1) every factual health statement has a source and date; (2) every claim is limited to what the evidence and app can support; (3) every external asset has a license or documented permission; (4) every person and testimonial is real, consented, and accurately described; (5) synthetic media is labeled where applicable; and (6) the CTA asks for a small next step rather than promising relief or a clinical result.

Measure the whole chain rather than views alone: attention/retention → profile or outbound click → qualified `/go` arrival → first use → signup or save when required → returning use. Existing YFIT figures in the brief have mixed windows and platform definitions, so they cannot establish a first-second drop-off or prove that articles caused low growth. Run a small, controlled comparison of original job-based demos versus article-inspired but fully original explainers. Hold topic, posting window, CTA, and destination as constant as practical; log source quality and disclosure; compare qualified arrivals and first-use events, not just views. Improvement thresholds are YFIT hypotheses to pre-register, not universal benchmarks.

**Conclusion.** Articles are useful inputs to editorial research, not copybait. Trust should come from relevance, accurate paraphrase, transparent sourcing, and a working product demonstration. This approach may trade a sensational claim for a smaller but better-qualified audience; whether that improves conversion remains an empirical YFIT question.
