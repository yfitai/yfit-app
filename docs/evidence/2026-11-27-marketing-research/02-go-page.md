# Go-page length, message match and demo-first conversion

## Finding

The current Go page is not merely a short social landing page. It is a feature catalogue, interactive preview, comparison page, and signup router in one long mobile document. That breadth is useful for visitors who already want to evaluate YFIT, but it creates a weak first-screen proposition for a social visitor arriving with one immediate question: “Can this app solve the job I just saw?” The evidence does not establish that a shorter page will automatically convert better. It does support a narrower first disclosure, a feature-specific entry path, and progressively available detail. The strongest YFIT hypothesis is therefore not “delete content”; it is “move most content out of the first decision and let the visitor try the promised job before asking for an account.”

## What the current source verifies

`GoPage.tsx` explicitly defines its flow as social post → `/go` → see all features → CTA, then signup or the full marketing site. The page’s hero claims “Every fitness feature you need” and lists workouts, nutrition, medications, AI coaching, form analysis, predictions, and habits. A top CTA appears before the feature list, and a second CTA appears at the bottom. The primary action is already “Explore the app first” and links to `https://app.yfitai.com/go?mode=guest`; signup and “Full Site” are secondary choices. The page therefore contains the seed of a demo-first strategy, but it asks the visitor to read a broad catalogue before the live exploration is available.

The page contains nine expandable feature cards, each with a title, paragraph, and hidden bullets. It then adds five app-preview tabs with a horizontally scrollable tab bar, mock content, and another “Try this feature live — no sign-up needed” link. Later sections add “Why YFIT is different,” a competitor table, and repeated offer language: “Free plan + 1 month Premium free — no credit card needed.” This is substantial scroll and interaction cost for a visitor who may have clicked from a single-problem social video. The expandable cards and tabs are not proof of poor usability; they are verified interaction and attention demands that should be tested rather than assumed to fail.

`LandingPage.tsx` is a different journey. Its hero offers signup, “See the app” scrolls to an in-page tour, and a smaller “Explore the app first — no account needed” link points to `/go`. Below that are an interactive app tour, Quick Setup/TDEE calculator, form-analysis and medication showcases, comparison tables, pricing plans, and several purchase or signup actions. Sending a social visitor from Go to this marketing page adds another promise, another navigation context, and eventually pricing/signup decisions. It may be appropriate for a high-intent evaluator, but it is an unverified extra step for a visitor who has not yet experienced the feature that motivated the click.

The source also verifies useful foundations. Go preserves UTM parameters into signup URLs and tracks page/CTA events through Umami when available. The guest path is explicit. The main landing page has Google sign-in elsewhere in the product context per the brief, but Google sign-in does not remove consent, privacy, identity, or first-use friction. No live browser test was performed, so there is no claim here about actual rendering, load speed, scroll depth, carousel usability, or current conversion.

## What credible evidence supports

Nielsen Norman Group defines progressive disclosure as showing only a few important options initially and revealing specialized options on request. It says this can improve learnability and efficiency, provided the initial/secondary split is correct and the path to more detail is obvious.[1] This maps closely to Go: one core job and one live proof should be initial; the nine-card catalogue, comparison, and full marketing detail can remain available behind clearly labeled choices. NN/g also warns that too many disclosure levels can make users lost, so the design should not become a maze of cards, tabs, and subpages.[1]

NN/g’s homepage guidance says visitors often scan rather than read, that the first screen should quickly communicate who the organization is and what users can accomplish, and that specific examples are more useful than broad umbrella terms.[2] It also recommends descriptive, goal-oriented CTA labels rather than generic “Explore” or “Learn more,” and warns that if everything is emphasized, nothing stands out.[2] For YFIT, “Explore the app first” is directionally honest but still broad. A feature-specific label such as “Try live form feedback” or “Scan a meal without an account” would carry more information scent when it matches the social post.

Google’s official Ads guidance does not say that shorter landing pages win. It says effective landing pages matter for conversions, mobile speed matters, and landing-page performance should be checked using actual interaction data; Google gives a retail example in which a one-second mobile delay can affect conversions by up to 20%.[3] That statistic is not a YFIT benchmark and should not be imported as a forecast. The actionable point is narrower: page length matters through the experience it creates, including loading and interaction cost, so measure qualified arrival, first use, and signup rather than assuming that fewer pixels equal higher conversion.

Baymard’s large-scale checkout research is not a SaaS landing-page study, so it is supporting evidence rather than a direct conversion estimate. It reports that 47% of sites that offer guest checkout fail to make it prominent, and that on mobile many participants failed to scroll far enough when guest access was outside the first viewport.[4] The mechanism is relevant to YFIT: if “no account needed” is a real value proposition, it must be visible and unmistakable at the first decision, not hidden after signup language. NN/g likewise advises thinking twice before forcing mobile users to register, making login optional where possible, offering social login, and asking for only the minimum information.[5]

A peer-reviewed study of landing-page text found an association between text readability features and conversion-rate prediction, but its authors explicitly state that it was not a conversion-optimization study. Its model used only 243 of 2,000 examples and requires more data for confirmation.[6] Use this as a caution against dense, difficult copy, not as evidence for a particular word count or a promised lift from shortening Go.

## Recommendation for Go and entry architecture

Use `/go` as a social intent router, not as a miniature full website. The first viewport should contain: the incoming problem in the visitor’s language; one concrete feature demonstration or interactive preview; one primary action to try that feature without signup; and a quiet secondary account path. Keep the offer and privacy expectation adjacent to the action. Do not lead with “all features” unless the acquisition creative itself promises a complete all-in-one app.

Create feature-specific entry states using a query or campaign mapping, for example `/go?feature=form`, `/go?feature=nutrition`, and `/go?feature=medications`. The first screen should preserve the exact promise of the social post, show one credible app outcome, and offer “Try [job] now — no account needed.” A compact “See everything YFIT includes” link can reveal the existing feature catalogue. Keep the existing full detail progressively available rather than deleting it. The destination can remain the guest mode already present in the source, with saves, personal health records, paid AI, and durable history gated only when those capabilities require identity or consent.

Test direct demo versus Go→marketing→signup as separate journeys. Variant A: social link goes to the feature-specific Go state and then directly to guest mode. Variant B: current Go page, with its first-screen CTA retained, sends interested users to guest mode. Variant C: Go sends visitors to the main marketing page before signup. Do not judge on signup rate alone: a direct demo may increase qualified product use while delaying registration, whereas a marketing page may create more immediate signup from a narrower high-intent segment. Compare qualified Go arrival, demo start, first meaningful action, signup completion, return visit, and paid or saved-feature intent. Use the existing UTM and event identity; do not add trackers or infer unique visitors from platform views.

The practical first test is low-volume and feature-matched: alternate comparable social posts or campaign links rather than changing every variable at once. Proposed thresholds and timing windows are YFIT hypotheses, not universal benchmarks. Before deciding, verify that guest mode works on the intended mobile path, that the feature shown is actually usable without signup, and that the gate explains what account creation unlocks. If the demo requires camera, medication, or personal health input, make permission, privacy, and safety boundaries explicit before collection. The honest offer should remain the existing free plan and clearly stated premium trial; do not use urgency or “only” claims unless they are substantiated.

## Limits and interpretation

There is no current Go traffic, scroll, event-funnel, or mobile performance export in this brief that would prove the page is too long or that visitors abandon in the first second. The current source shows potential friction and message breadth, not observed failure. The social metrics supplied elsewhere are differently scoped and cannot substitute for qualified Go arrivals or retention. “Shorter is better” is therefore an unverified outcome hypothesis. “Narrow first screen, exact message match, visible guest demo, progressive detail, and measurement of activation before signup” is the evidence-backed design direction.
